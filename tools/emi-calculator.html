About the EMI & Loan Amortization Calculator

This existing combined EMI and Loan Amortization Calculator already supports multiple repayment methods and detailed installment schedules. The enhancement keeps its existing calculation interface and JavaScript intact while adding clearer method notes, a visual repayment flow, relevant references and tool-specific FAQs.

How to use

  1. Enter the principal, rate or markup basis, term and payment period.
  2. Choose a repayment method and review periodic payment, interest and remaining balance.
  3. Check the full schedule and total repayment; lender fees, insurance and late charges may be additional.

Formula and method

For a standard reducing-balance loan with periodic rate r and n payments, EMI = P × r × (1+r)^n ÷ [(1+r)^n − 1]. Zero-rate loans use principal divided by the number of payments. Equal-principal and flat-markup methods follow different schedules.

Worked comparison

Compare like-for-like principal, rate, payment frequency and term when reviewing EMI, equal-principal and flat-markup schedules; a quoted rate alone is not enough to compare total cost.

Assumptions and limitations

Confirm payment frequency, rate convention, fees, insurance and lender terms. This tool is for comparison and education, not a lender quote or personalized financial advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is EMI?

Equated monthly installment is a periodic payment under a specified loan method; the exact schedule depends on rate, term and payment conventions.

How is reducing balance different from flat markup?

Reducing-balance interest is calculated on the remaining principal; flat markup commonly applies the rate to the original principal for the agreed period.

Can a lower installment still cost more?

Yes. A longer term can reduce the periodic installment while increasing total paid or total financing cost.

Are fees and taxes included?

Only fields shown in the calculator are included. Processing fees, insurance, late charges and legal taxes may be extra.

Is this a lender quote?

No. Use the schedule to compare assumptions and verify the final terms with the lender.
